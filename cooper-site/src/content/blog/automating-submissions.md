Ask a commercial broker where their week goes and you will not hear "advising clients." You will hear about re-keying dec pages, chasing loss runs, filling the same ACORD supplement for the fourth time this month, and logging into a carrier portal that logs you back out.

None of that is the job. All of it is the week.

That gap is why we wrote the white paper, and it is why this blog exists. Over the next few months we will publish what we learn building AI for this work: benchmarks and engineering notes, where the industry is heading, and brokers describing their own days. This is the first post, and the paper it points to is the argument underneath all of them.

## The bottleneck is not judgement

The instinct when people hear "AI for insurance" is that something is coming for underwriting judgement. That is the wrong thing to worry about and, more practically, the wrong thing to build.

Judgement is a small share of the hours. The large share is mechanical: moving structured information out of one document and into another, in a format some carrier specified years ago. It is exacting, it is repetitive, it does not benefit from experience, and it is the reason good brokers spend evenings on work a first-year could do.

That is the part worth automating first. Not because it is easy, but because it is where the hours actually are.

## Drafting is not finishing

Most tools that reach a brokerage today stop one step short. They will draft a submission, summarise a loss run, or pre-fill a form, and then hand it back for a human to carry the rest of the way.

That is not nothing. But it moves the work rather than removing it. If a broker still has to open the carrier portal, log in, re-enter what the tool just produced, and handle the supplement that portal demands, the time saved is a fraction of the time promised. The last mile is where the estimate meets reality.

We think the honest test of any tool in this category is simple: does it finish, or does it hand back? Everything else follows from that.

## Seven questions worth asking

The paper works through seven criteria for evaluating a tool in this space. They are written to be used in a vendor conversation rather than admired in the abstract, and several are uncomfortable to ask, which is the point.

They cover things like where a tool stops and a human resumes, what happens when a carrier changes a form, how the system behaves when it is uncertain rather than wrong, and what evidence exists that any of it works on your book rather than a demo account.

You do not need our product to use them. You need them more if you are evaluating someone else's.

## What this blog will be

Three tracks, rotating, one post every Thursday.

**Engineering** is how the thing is built and how we measure whether it works, including the numbers that are not flattering. **State of Insurance** is where the industry is going and what that means for a brokerage deciding when to move. **Industry Insider** is brokers on their own work, in their own words, including the parts where our software annoyed them.

We would rather publish something specific and occasionally awkward than something polished and empty. That is a low bar to set publicly, which is why we are setting it here in the first post.

The full paper covers the workflow end to end, from intake through renewal, and includes the seven criteria in full.
