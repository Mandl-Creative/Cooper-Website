Renewal prep is the work nobody puts on a calendar and everybody does anyway. Pull the expiring policy, pull the loss runs, rebuild the exposure schedule, re-key it into whatever the carrier wants this year, and hope the client has not added three vehicles since March.

A commercial agency running about four hundred accounts walked us through what that looked like before, what they handed over first, and the part they still do by hand.

## Before

Three hours per renewal was the honest average, and it clustered badly. The week before a large account renewed, one producer would lose most of two days to it.

The three hours were not spent thinking. They were spent finding. The expiring policy was in the AMS, the loss runs were in an inbox, the last exposure schedule was in a spreadsheet on somebody's desktop, and the current vehicle list was in an email the client sent in a format nobody asked for.

## What they handed over first

Not the judgement calls. The retrieval.

They started with loss-run collection and the exposure schedule rebuild, which are the two most mechanical steps and the two that most reliably eat an afternoon. The instruction internally was that the output still went to a human before it went to a carrier, and that has not changed.

## What changed

Renewal prep now runs about fifteen minutes of review on a typical account. The prep itself happens before anyone sits down.

The number that mattered more to them was not the fifteen minutes. It was that the work stopped clustering. Large renewals no longer consume the two days before they are due, which means the producer is available for the conversation the client actually wants.

## The part they still do by hand

Coverage changes. Anything where the answer depends on knowing the client, the market, or what the carrier will actually accept this quarter stays with a person, and they are not looking to change that.

Their words, roughly: the software got the afternoon back, and the afternoon is where the account management was supposed to happen.
